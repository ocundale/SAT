*Tens of millions of pheasants are released into Britain's woods every year. As the government asks, for the first time, what that does to the countryside, a walk in south Cumbria suggests the question is overdue.*

The first pheasant was a pleasure. By the thirtieth I had stopped counting.

I was walking through old woodland near Dallam Tower, south of Milnthorpe, where limestone breaks through the leaf litter on ground that has been wooded for at least four centuries. The pheasants were everywhere. They trotted ahead of me down the path, stood about in clearings like poultry and burst from the undergrowth every few yards. *\[Add your own details here: the date, the route, what you saw and heard.\]*

It did not feel like a wood with birds in it. It felt like a wood with something added to it, in bulk. I went home wanting to know where they had all come from, and what so many large, hungry, ground-feeding birds might be doing to a place that old.

The answer turns out to be one of the stranger facts about the British countryside. It is also, as of this autumn, a matter of government inquiry.

## Somewhere between 31 and 50 million

The pheasant is not a native bird. It has bred wild here since around the 12th century, long enough to seem part of the furniture. But the birds I met were almost certainly not wild-bred. Most pheasants in Britain today are hatched in incubators, reared in captivity and put into woodland pens in late summer at about two months old, to be shot between October and February.

How many? Nobody knows, because nobody is required to count. The most careful academic estimate, by [Joah Madden of the University of Exeter](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10344-021-01508-z), is 31.5 million pheasants a year. The shooting sector's own research body has put it [nearer 47 million](https://cairngorms.co.uk/documents/scottish-gamebird-releases-report/html). Add around nine or ten million red-legged partridges, also non-native.

Weigh them and the picture sharpens. The British Trust for Ornithology calculates that released gamebirds amount to [about 45,000 tonnes](https://www.bto.org/community/blog/what-effect-might-annual-releases-non-native-gamebirds-be-having-native-biodiversity) of bird, more than twice the combined weight of every native bird breeding in Britain. At the late-summer peak, by one estimate, pheasants and partridges make up about half of all the wild bird biomass in Britain.

Most never meet a gun. Around [60% of released pheasants are not shot](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10344-018-1199-5). In [radio-tracking studies](https://gwct.org.uk/research/species/birds/common-pheasant/fate-of-released-pheasants/), more than a third were eaten, mostly by foxes. Others starve, sicken or die on the road: pheasants make up [almost 7% of Britain's reported roadkill](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/news/view/968315-pheasants-on-britains-roads).

And the scale has crept up. The density of birds released per site [rose by 4.3% a year](https://community.rspb.org.uk/cfs-file/__key/telligent-evolution-components-attachments/01-248396-00-00-00-79-62-37/Gamebird-evidence-review.pdf) from 1960 to 2014. About one English wood in 12 now has a release pen in it.

## What a pen does to a wood

The clearest damage is the least disputed, because the shooting world's own scientists documented it.

A release pen is an open-topped enclosure in a wood, often holding thousands of young birds for weeks. Researchers from what is now the Game & Wildlife Conservation Trust [surveyed pens in 43 ancient woodlands](https://gwct.org.uk/research/scientific-publications/2000-09/2005/sage2005/) and found what you might expect of ground that crowded: more bare earth, fewer of the shade-loving perennials that mark out old woodland, more weeds that thrive on enriched, disturbed soil.

The effects outlast the birds. A follow-up study of abandoned pens found the soil still richer and the woodland flowers still poorer years later. Ancient woodland plants began to return after a decade, but [only where fewer than 1,000 birds per hectare had been kept](https://gwct.org.uk/research/scientific-publications/2010-19/2019/capstick2019/). Because keepers sometimes move pens to control disease, the authors warned, the damage can add up across a wood.

That figure of 1,000 birds per hectare of pen is now the industry's own guideline, dropping to 700 in ancient woodland. It is guidance, not law. When government scientists tracked birds from 11 pens on working shoots in 2023, [six held more than 1,000 per hectare](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.11.705276v1.full). One held 3,614.

Away from the pens the picture is gentler. A recent Natural England study of 20 ancient woods found [no significant change in soil nutrients or woodland indicator plants](https://publications.naturalengland.org.uk/publication/4550555761115136) with distance from the pen. But a companion study, looking up instead of down, found the lichens and mosses on tree trunks shifting towards nitrogen-tolerant species [as far as 250 metres away](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.08.723433.full.pdf). Lichens feed from the air. Thousands of birds, it seems, change the air.

## Beyond the fence

The harder question is what happens once the birds walk out. Here the evidence is newer, thinner and fiercely argued over.

**They go further than the rules assume.** England licenses pheasant release only on or within 500 metres of its most protected wildlife sites, on the official view that birds mostly stay within that distance. This year the government's Animal and Plant Health Agency published GPS tracks from 110 pheasants that say otherwise. [Nearly three quarters went beyond 500 metres](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.11.705276v1.full). A quarter entered a protected site, some from pens more than 1.5 km away. One bird travelled almost nine kilometres. The more crowded the pen, the further they roamed.

**They feed the predators.** A third of released pheasants end up inside something else, and that is a lot of free meat. The BTO has found [more buzzards, jays and crows](https://www.bto.org/our-work/science/publications/papers/associations-between-gamebird-releases-and-general) where gamebirds are abundant. A study of 117 foxes in southern England estimated that gamebirds made up [about 43% of the diet](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/isotopic-evidence-for-the-consumption-of-galliformes-by-the-red-fox-ivulpes-vulpesi-in-rural-southern-england/) on shoots. The fear is what those well-fed predators eat in spring, when the pheasants are gone and curlews and lapwings are on their nests. That link has not been proven. Shoots also kill foxes, and one national analysis found [fewer foxes reported](https://www.gwct.org.uk/research/paper-summaries/is-there-more-wildlife-on-gamebird-release-sites) where gamebirds are released, not more.

**They may carry disease.** Researchers at Exeter and the UK Health Security Agency found the bacteria that cause Lyme disease in [7.8% of ticks in pheasant-release woods, against 3.2%](https://news.exeter.ac.uk/?p=19651) in woods nearby. Nobody has yet shown more people falling ill. Bird flu is the sharper worry for regulators: for 2026 there is [no general licence](https://www.nationalgamekeepers.org.uk/articles/natural-england-tightens-gamebird-release-rules-for-2026) to release gamebirds near sites protected for wild birds.

Some popular claims fare worse. You will read that pheasants are eating Britain's adders to extinction. There are anecdotes of pecked snakes, but [no reptile remains turned up](https://gwct.org.uk/blogs/news/2022/august/a-look-at-the-impact-of-gamebird-release-on-reptiles/) in studies of more than 2,500 pheasant diets. It may be true. It has not been shown.

## The keeper's case

It would be easy to stop there. It would also be unfair.

A wood kept for pheasants is a wood somebody is paying attention to. Keepers thin the canopy, widen the rides and encourage the shrubby edges pheasants like, and other things like them too. A survey of [159 lowland woods](https://gwct.org.uk/research/scientific-publications/2000-09/2008/draycott2008/) found those managed for pheasants had denser ground vegetation and more songbirds, warblers especially. Game crops planted on farmland feed finches through the winter. Many small woods and hedges exist today only because someone once wanted somewhere to hold birds.

There is money in it as well. A report commissioned by shooting organisations values shooting of all kinds at [£3.3 billion a year](https://www.thefield.co.uk/sponsored/the-value-of-shooting-report-2024-52483) and credits it with conservation work on 7.6 million hectares. The government is commissioning its own figures.

Even the critics grant some of this. When the RSPB reviewed the science in 2020 it accepted clear benefits from habitat management, while concluding that the evidence of harm from the birds themselves was [larger and growing](https://community.rspb.org.uk/ourwork/b/science/posts/reviewing-the-science-on-the-impacts-of-gamebird-releasing-what-happens-when-two-groups-of-scientists-review-the-same-evidence). The Game & Wildlife Conservation Trust read the same studies and [came out far more favourably](https://gwct.org.uk/blogs/news/2020/october/reviewing-the-science-on-the-effects-of-gamebird-releasing-%E2%80%93-same-evidence,-different-conclusions/).

The fair summary is that both things are true at once. Good management helps wildlife. Too many birds harms it. A small shoot releasing a few hundred pheasants into well-kept woodland and a commercial one putting down tens of thousands are not the same activity, and at present the law treats them alike.

## A question finally asked

For most of the country there are no rules on how many pheasants may be released, or where. The exception dates from 2020, when the campaign group Wild Justice took the government to court and [Defra conceded](https://www.leighday.co.uk/news/press-releases/2020-news/defra-announces-protection-of-wildlife-sites-from-release-of-millions-of-gamebirds/) days before the hearing. The pheasant joined the list of species that need a licence to release, alongside the grey squirrel, but only on or within 500 metres of sites with European protection.

That may be about to change. In March the government's Land Use Framework said it would explore licensing gamebird shooting and release everywhere. On 4 September Defra opened a [call for evidence](https://consultations.defra.gov.uk/call-for-evidence-on-gamebird-release-shooting-and-welfare-during-rearing-in-england) on the effects of rearing, releasing and shooting gamebirds: on wildlife and protected habitats, disease, wildlife crime and the welfare of the birds themselves, which are not covered by the rules that protect farmed poultry. It closes on 1 November. Wales began a similar review in the spring.

The reaction has been loud. The British Association for Shooting and Conservation called it [a fight for the future of shooting](https://www.fwi.co.uk/news/defra-launches-gamebird-shooting-call-for-evidence). The Countryside Alliance called it an unnecessary and unjustified attack. The RSPB, which says it is neutral on the ethics of shooting, [hopes it is a first step](https://www.rspb.org.uk/whats-happening/news/the-government-takes-positive-steps-towards-bird-of-prey-protections) towards licences that an estate could lose if its keepers kill birds of prey.

Nothing has been decided, and any proposal would need a public consultation. But the most useful thing licensing could do is also the least dramatic. It would make someone write the numbers down.

## Back in the wood

I do not know whose pheasants I walked among, how many were released nearby or how tightly they were penned. *\[If the estate or a local keeper will talk to you, their answer goes here, and it may change this ending.\]* That is rather the point. Neither does anyone else, because no one has to say.

What can be said is that this corner of Cumbria is the kind of place where it matters. Cumbria Wildlife Trust names the [estate woodlands around Morecambe Bay](https://www.cumbriawildlifetrust.org.uk/sites/default/files/2021-11/Gamebirds%20and%20Recreational%20Shooting.pdf) as among the most heavily managed for gamebirds in the county. The limestone woods here, with their small-leaved lime and yew, are [protected at the highest level](https://sac.jncc.gov.uk/site/UK0014777). More than 40% of the area's woodland is ancient.

A pheasant is a handsome bird and I do not grudge anyone the sight of one. But a wood is a slow thing. Its flowers take a decade to come back to a patch of trampled ground, if they come back at all. It seems little to ask that before tens of millions of birds are put into such places each summer, somebody should know how many, and where, and be able to say when it is too much.
